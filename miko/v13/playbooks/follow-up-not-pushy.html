The Follow-Up That Isn't Pushy

A cadence that keeps you welcome in their messages — and books the ones who were always going to book.

A Miko Pierson playbook · Bigger Than Shears™

Most stylists follow up once and call it follow-up — if they follow up at all.

Not because they're lazy. Because they don't want to be the message someone screenshots as annoying. The fear is real — but it's aimed at the wrong thing. What makes follow-up feel pushy is stacking — five messages in three days that all say "just checking in." Spaced out, permissioned follow-up is read as professionalism. The data backs the cadence — and names the line where persistence stops paying.

The three facts that shape the cadence

  • They prefer the text. Large consumer surveys keep landing the same answer: roughly half to three-quarters of clients prefer appointment reminders by text, and texting pulls response rates far above calls and email. Your follow-up belongs where the conversation already lives.
  • Spacing beats stacking. Sales-cadence data shows follow-ups sent roughly three days apart pull about a third more replies than next-day bumps. Persistence isn't frequency — it's presence at the right intervals.
  • There's a natural stopping point. Large-dataset sales research puts the real conversion curve at about six contacts — beyond that, effort climbs and odds don't. A cadence with an end is a system. A cadence without one is a spiral.

The 6-touch cadence

Each touch carries something for them — never just "checking in." Checking in takes; bringing something gives.

  1. Day 0 — the close of the visit. "Same chair, four weeks — I'll hold it." (If they left unbooked: "Sending your upkeep notes now — easier to book early than to need it late.")
  2. Day 3 — value, not a nudge. Care tip or photo of their finish: "The silver held beautifully — keep it cool-toned with a violet wash once a week."
  3. Day 7–10 — the helpful window. "Your maintenance window lands around the 14th — want me to set it?"
  4. Day 14–17 — one honest line. "Last window I'm holding open for this cycle — want it or want to skip this round?" (An exit is an acceptable answer; a graceful close earns the next yes.)
  5. Day 30 — a reason to return. Seasonal note, new service, or a client-exclusive: give the touch a payload, not just a ping.
  6. Seasonal — the long tail. Three touches a year at the moments that matter (holiday rush, summer, back-to-school). Low pressure, permanent presence.

The rules that keep it welcome

  1. Ask permission once. "Text okay for reminders?" — one question at booking, then a channel they chose. Research on consumer messaging preference is blunt: people want to text AND talk, on their terms — a call they didn't expect reads as an intrusion; a text they opted into reads as service.
  2. Every touch gives something. A tip, a reminder window, a held slot, an exclusive. If the message only asks, it's a debt collector.
  3. Stop means stop. Two unanswered touches in a row → they move to the seasonal track. Restraint is part of the brand.

A stat to skip

You'll see "80% of sales need five follow-ups" quoted everywhere — including in this industry's seminars. Nobody can produce the study. Quote the real numbers instead: most businesses stop after one contact, and the conversions live in touches two through six. You don't need a fake statistic to justify a real cadence.

Follow-up isn't chasing — it's holding the door open at the right intervals. Who on your list deserves the door held for them this week?
